How to read this page
Solar panels generate electricity from sunlight. When the sun sets, no light hits the panels, so no electricity is produced. This is completely normal — the plant will start producing again the next morning when the sun rises, typically around 6:00 am.
Think of it like water. kW (kilowatt) is how fast water is flowing — the flow rate. kWh (kilowatt-hour) is how much water you collected in total over time — the volume. A 10 kW plant running for 1 hour produces 10 kWh. Your electricity bill is measured in kWh (called "units").
The system compares actual output with expected output based on live sunshine data from a weather service. If the actual output falls below 50% of expected for 3 readings in a row, and sunshine is above 150 W/m², a problem is flagged. Below that sunshine level (night or heavy cloud), no alert is raised — low power in those conditions is completely normal.
The power and energy readings come directly from the inverter, which measures the electricity your panels are producing. Sunshine data comes from the Open-Meteo weather service. Expected output is calculated using a standard solar engineering formula — it is a model, not a physical measurement.
Money saved = electricity generated (kWh) × electricity tariff (₹7/kWh). CO₂ avoided = electricity generated × grid emission factor (0.7 kg/kWh). These are estimates — actual savings depend on your real electricity contract and the local grid's fuel mix.
